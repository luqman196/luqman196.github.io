Profitable on paper,
rejected at the bank.

20 September 2026 · Kredit Lab · 5 min read

A company turns a healthy profit three years running, applies for a working capital line, and is declined. The letter says the application "does not meet our credit criteria at this time." Nobody explains which criteria. Here is what was almost certainly on the screen.

He Had the Numbers. The Bank Still Said No. More on YouTube ↗

This is the most common conversation we have. The owner is genuinely baffled, and reasonably so: the business made money, the accounts are audited, the auditor signed them without qualification. From the inside, the case looks obvious.

From the credit officer's side of the desk it looks like something else entirely — because the question a bank is asking is not the question the profit and loss statement answers.

Profit is an opinion. Cash is a fact. A bank lends against the second one.

Your P&L records a sale when you raise the invoice. It is a perfectly correct statement about what your business earned. But a loan is not repaid out of earnings — it is repaid out of money that has actually arrived in an account. So the credit officer spends most of their time converting your profit back into cash, and then asking whether what is left can service the repayment you are requesting, on top of everything you already owe.

Three gaps do most of the damage.

1. Growth that is sitting in somebody else's bank account

Revenue is up forty per cent. Good. Trade receivables are up seventy per cent. Less good — it means you grew by selling to customers who have not paid yet, and you funded the gap yourself.

The credit officer works out roughly how many days of sales are stuck in receivables, and watches whether that number is getting worse year on year. If it is, the growth is real but the cash is not, and lending into it just enlarges the hole. Inventory behaves the same way: stock is an asset on the balance sheet and a hostage in real life.

This single mechanism is why a fast-growing company can be more worrying to a lender than a flat one.

2. What you already owe, added to what you are asking for

Every existing commitment gets pulled together — term loans, hire purchase, trade lines, leases — and turned into one number: what the business must pay out over the next twelve months, principal included. Not the interest alone. The whole repayment.

That number is compared against the cash the business genuinely generates from operations. Then the facility you have applied for is added to the pile, and the comparison is run again.

Plenty of profitable SMEs fail at exactly this step, and almost none of them saw it coming, because the repayment obligations are scattered across half a dozen agreements and nobody has ever added them up in one place. The bank adds them up. Always.

3. The director's account

Money moves between owner and company in most Malaysian SMEs. It is normal, it is usually innocent, and it is read very carefully.

An amount due from a director is, to a credit officer, cash that left the business and has not come back — capital the company raised or earned, now sitting somewhere the bank has no claim over. An amount due to a director is treated more kindly, but it invites the follow-up question of whether that money will be pulled out the moment the facility is drawn. Either way, the movement gets noticed, and a large unexplained one can quietly cost you the application.

Then they read your bank statements

Your audited accounts might be fifteen months old. Your bank statements are from last week — which is why, for a working capital assessment, they often carry more weight than the audited file.

What gets read there:

  • Whether the revenue you declared actually appears as credits into the account, or whether the two numbers live in different universes.
  • How many counterparties your money comes from. One customer paying for everything is a concentration risk, however good that customer is.
  • Whether the balance is comfortable or perpetually near zero before each payroll run.
  • Returned items and bounced instructions — a handful of these can outweigh a good P&L, because they speak to conduct rather than capacity.
  • Whether inflows look like trade at all, or like funds circulating between related accounts.

None of these are exotic. They are simply invisible to most owners, because nobody reads their own bank statements the way a stranger with a lending decision would.

Why nobody tells you any of this

It is not malice, and it is rarely incompetence. A decline letter is a risk document, not a coaching document. A detailed explanation creates a negotiation, a paper trail, and sometimes a complaint. So the letter says nothing, the relationship manager is sympathetic but vague, and the business owner is left to guess — usually guessing wrong, and often reapplying six months later with the same numbers and a nicer folder.

What you can do before you apply

Three things, none of which require anyone's permission:

  • Add up every repayment. All facilities, twelve months forward, principal and profit or interest together. Compare it honestly against the cash your operations actually threw off last year. If the answer is uncomfortable, the bank will reach it too.
  • Reconcile revenue to your bank. Take last year's declared revenue and find it in your account credits. Where the two do not meet, understand why before someone else asks.
  • Read six months of statements as a stranger. Not for the balance. For the pattern — who pays you, how often, how fast it leaves again.

None of this guarantees an approval. Nothing does: collateral, credit history, industry conditions and management all carry weight, and some of them are outside your control entirely. But it changes the question you walk in with. "We don't know why they said no" becomes "we know which number is the problem, and whether it can be fixed before we ask again."

Your business already has a file at the bank. There is no rule that says you have to be the last person to read it.
